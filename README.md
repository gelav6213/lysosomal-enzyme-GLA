## Answers

1. What does your enzyme normally break down inside lysosomes?
- Alpha-galactosidase A is a lysosomal enzyme that breaks down certain glycolipids, particularly globotriaosylceramide (Gb3). It helps cells degrade these substances and maintain normal cellular function.
  
2. What is the total length of your protein, and which amino acids are most common?
- The protein contains 470 amino acids. The most common amino acids are L, A, G, D, and S, based on the amino acid composition results generated by the R script.
  
3. How many potential N-glycosylation motifs did you find? Where are they located?
- The protein sequence contains 4 potential N-glycosylation motifs. These motifs are located at amino acid positions 104 (NYS), 180 (NKT), 233 (NRT), and 256 (NYT). They match the N-X-S/T sequence pattern, where X can be any amino acid except proline.

4. Why can glycosylation matter for lysosomal proteins? Does finding a motif prove the protein is glycosylated?
- Glycosylation helps lysosomal proteins fold properly, stay stable, and reach the correct location in the cell. Some lysosomal enzymes also have mannose-6-phosphate tags that help direct them to lysosomes. However, finding an N-X-S/T motif does not necessarily mean that the protein is glycosylated because the motif may not be modified inside the cell. Sequence analysis alone cannot confirm glycosylation or the presence of a mannose-6-phosphate tag.

5. What could happen in the cell if your enzyme did not work properly?
- If alpha-galactosidase A does not function properly, its substrate, particularly globotriaosylceramide (Gb3), can accumulate in lysosomes. This accumulation can disrupt normal cellular function and contribute to Fabry disease.
